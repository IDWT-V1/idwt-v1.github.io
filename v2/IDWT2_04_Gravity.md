# Infinite Dimensional Wave Theory, Second Edition — Part 4: Gravity

## 1. Gravity is what mass does to the manifold

Gravity in IDWT is not a force in Part 3's sense and not a field in anyone's sense. It is the curvature of $M_\infty$ itself, sourced by mass — by the concentrated energy density of the wave. Where the wave holds a stable standing wave, the surrounding geometry is distorted; that distortion is gravity, complete. There are no gravitons, no spin-2 quanta, and nothing to quantize, because there is no gravitational field — only geometry responding to mass. The century-old program of "quantizing gravity" dissolves here not because it is hard but because its subject does not exist: one does not quantize a shape.

The direction of explanation matters. The familiar Einstein equations are, in this framework, the *observer's reconstruction*: a three-dimensional being, measuring the curvature of $M_\infty$ at its own coordinate level, writes down an effective field equation sourced by the masses it sees, and that equation works for every practical purpose. But it is a description of the measurement, not of the phenomenon. The phenomenon is a definite curvature assigned to a definite mass distribution — linear, covariant, inheriting every symmetry of its source exactly. The pathologies that haunt the field-theoretic reading — solution branches, singularity theorems as statements about reality, metric non-uniqueness — belong to the reconstruction, not the physics. Where a mass concentration is dense enough that the observer's effective description develops a horizon, the underlying geometry stays regular; what reads as a singularity from three dimensions is a finite-energy, high-amplitude region of the wave.

## 2. Why enormous flat dimensions pass every experimental test

The framework claims macroscopic, infinite, flat hidden dimensions — and physics has spent decades setting exquisite bounds on extra dimensions. Torsion-balance tests find no deviation from the inverse-square law below fifty microns; colliders see no missing energy from gravitons escaping into the bulk; precision spectroscopy shows no extra-dimensional shifts. How do the sectors survive?

Because every one of those bounds tests the same hypothesis, and it is not this one. The excluded scenario is Kaluza–Klein: compact extra dimensions into which gravitons propagate, producing a tower of massive graviton modes and a force law that steepens below the compactification radius. IDWT contains none of that machinery. There are no gravitons to escape anywhere; there is no compact radius and hence no tower; the sector-space geometry is not a dynamical field with its own fluctuations. A particle's activity in its sector directions is a Gaussian-localized standing wave, not a plane wave — nothing propagates through the sector space to be missed or detected. The bounds do not constrain the sectors because the thing they measure was never present.

And the sectors are not experimentally invisible — the opposite. They produce the entire particle mass spectrum (Part 2). What they do not produce is any *additional gravitational* signature beyond the masses of the particles they host, which is exactly what is observed.

The inverse-square law itself gets the same treatment here as the Coulomb law did in Part 3: a source interacting in $d$ dimensions has a $1/r^{d-2}$ potential, but a three-dimensional observer integrates over the hidden coordinates and lands on exactly $1/r$ — for every sector. Newtonian gravity is not evidence of three dimensions; it is what higher-dimensional gravity looks like to a three-dimensional resident.

## 3. Bound within, gradient-free without

The single most consequential fact about IDWT gravity follows from Part 1's "bound within, free without." A mass curves the manifold with a *gradient* — a pull — only in the dimensions it is localized in. In every direction where a source is uniform, the geometry it sources is translation-invariant along that direction: uniform source, no gradient, no pull. Three consequences:

**Each object gravitates in its own dimensions.** The electron sources a gravitational gradient in six dimensions; the tau in ten; a nucleon in three. Gravity is not confined to our slice — it is confined, source by source, to wherever each source is bound.

**Ordinary gravity is three-dimensional because ordinary matter is.** Nucleons — and everything built from them, planets, stars, observers — are purely three-dimensionally bound (the colour-singlet argument of Part 1). Two such objects source and feel gradients only in the shared three dimensions, which is ordinary inverse-square gravity. We measure 3D gravity because our sources are 3D, not because gravity is.

**Nothing hides from gravity, and nothing is diluted.** By the nesting, every massive mode is bound in $d=3$; against a three-dimensional source it pulls with its complete scalar mass through the shared coordinates — never a projected fraction, never a reduced part. A higher-sector object is gravitationally an ordinary point mass to us, however much structure it carries in coordinates we cannot see. Structurally, then, the universe naturally admits objects that gravitate at full strength while being otherwise nearly invisible to three-dimensional instruments. Whether this accounts for the observed dark matter is an open question the framework owns rather than papers over: the fifteen-particle spectrum is complete, no specific dark-matter candidate is currently derived, and the cosmological abundance question is untouched. The geometry makes gravitationally-dark structure natural; it has not yet produced a candidate.

There is also a clean answer to why gravity's reach is finite in dimension: every source is uniform beyond its own sector, and every sector stops at $d=10$, so nothing sources a gradient in the dimensions beyond ten. They are Ricci-flat and silent — not truncated, just empty.

## 4. Newton's constant is a measurement, not a fundamental coupling

The manifold has one gravitational stiffness, $G_\infty$ — curvature per unit mass. What a three-dimensional observer measures is that stiffness read through their own geometry: integrating any source over its hidden coordinates leaves a Newtonian potential whose strength is

$$G_N = \frac{G_\infty}{4\pi},$$

and the $4\pi$ is the area of the *observer's* unit sphere — the signature of the measurer's three dimensions, not of the source's. The remarkable part is the sector-independence: the hidden-coordinate integral produces a prefactor that exactly cancels the $d$-dimensional normalization of the source's own Green's function, identically for every sector. A $d=6$ electron and a $d=10$ tau pull on us with the same $G_N$ per unit mass as a $d=3$ quark. The equivalence principle is not a postulate here; inertial and gravitational mass are the same sector eigenvalue read twice, so their ratio is one for every species, with no fifth force and no composition dependence — as measured.

One striking cross-connection: gravity sets the size of elementary particles. A particle is a stiffness-bound standing wave, and the stiffness of the space it stands in is $1/G_\infty$; working out the balance gives every elementary mode a physical size near $10^{-29}$–$10^{-30}$ m — pointlike far below any resolved scale, comfortably clearing all compositeness bounds, with heavier modes *smaller* (as the fourth root of inverse mass). The femtometre scales of nuclear physics are composite extents of confined quark systems, not single-particle sizes. So the two dimensional inputs of the framework divide the labor cleanly: the electron mass prices the spectrum, and $G_\infty$ sizes the particles.

## 5. The honest boundaries

**The strength of gravity is an input.** The framework derives gravity's *structure* — sourced curvature, sector-independence, $G_N = G_\infty/4\pi$, the equivalence principle — but not its *magnitude*. $G_\infty$ is a second dimensional input alongside $m_e$, a different kind of quantity, and the two are never compared: there is no hierarchy problem, because the wave's kernel couplings and the manifold's stiffness are not two entries on one scale that mysteriously differ, but two categories with no ratio between them. Reducing two inputs to one — a geometric determination of $G_\infty$ — would be a genuine deepening and is not available in the present construction.

**The cosmological constant is open.** The dimensions beyond $d=10$ contribute exactly zero vacuum energy — they host no normalizable modes and carry no condensate, so their silence is a result, not an assumption. But the occupied sectors' condensates carry real vacuum energy, and why the observed cosmological term is as small as it is has no derivation here yet. The problem is confined, not solved.

**Cosmology generally is early.** The framework's gravity is a statement about sources and geometry, not yet a cosmological history: relic abundances, structure formation, and the expansion record are programs, not results. Part 6 lists what observations would falsify the gravitational picture as it stands.

What is *not* open, and worth restating as the Part closes: within the solar-system and laboratory regime where gravity is actually measured, this picture reproduces everything — Newtonian gravity with the measured constant, exact equivalence, no fifth force, no force-law deviation at any tested scale — from the single statement that mass curves the manifold it occupies, and that we are three-dimensional readers of it.
