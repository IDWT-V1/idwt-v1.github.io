## 1. Why the quantum world is discrete

The wave is continuous. The clicks are not. The reconciliation needs two facts, one about detectors and one about the wave.

**Detectors are bound matter, and bound spectra are discrete.** A detector's stable configurations are sector modes with finite level gaps (Part 1 §1.3: the wells have purely discrete spectra). Any registration is a kernel interaction that moves the detector between two of its own discrete levels — so whatever the wave delivers, the *record* is a discrete level change. Continuous intensity, read out through a discrete instrument, arrives in counts. This much is bookkeeping any wave theory could claim.

**The size of one count is the quantum of action — the framework's one import.** What the classical field does not supply on its own is why a transition completes as a whole unit. The framework is unusually candid here because it has a proof: rescaling the wave and coupling together ($\Psi \to c\Psi$, $g \to g/c^2$) changes *no* dimensionless observable — every mass ratio, angle, and size is invariant — while rescaling all absolute rates. So no observable of the classical theory can fix the amplitude of "one excitation"; the quantum is *provably underivable* from the framework's own axioms, and it enters as a single stated import: a stable excitation carries unit conserved charge. The number is one; nothing is fitted; and through this single door pass all the things quantum mechanics attributes to $\hbar$ — the discreteness of exchange, absolute decay rates, the scale of one click. Everything else in the framework (masses, angles, chemistry, all rate *ratios*) is independent of it.

That division — discreteness of the *record* from detector spectra, discreteness of the *exchange* from one imported quantum — is the complete account of "quantization" here. There is no quantized field; there is a classical wave, discrete instruments, and one unit.

Decay works the same way and is worth a paragraph, because lifetimes are where wave mechanics meets the clock. A sector excitation does not "decide" to decay; its amplitude leaks through the kernel into modes it couples to, and irreversibly into the one true continuum available — spatial radiation. The leak rate is the classical resonance-width formula $\Gamma = 2\pi|g|^2\rho$, computed from the kernel matrix element and the density of radiation states — no second quantization, no scattering formalism. Run end-to-end for the muon (the weak vertex from Part 3, the derived $G_F$, the three-body phase-space integral done natively), it reproduces the measured lifetime through $\Gamma_\mu = G_F^2m_\mu^5/192\pi^3$ — the famous fifth-power law arriving as classical wave mechanics. Which particles are absolutely stable also falls out: the photon, up quark (in isolation), electron, and the three neutrinos — each because every downward door its geometry offers is closed by a filter, a parity, or the absence of anything lighter to leak into.

## 2. The atom, actually

Assemble the pieces already derived. The nucleus is a colour-singlet composite — purely three-dimensional (Part 1 §5.1), and *geometrically thin* in what follows. The electron is a six-dimensional standing wave executing a definite closed orbit in its sector geometry, bound to the nucleus through the two-dimensional electromagnetic plane both contain, feeling — by the projection theorem of Part 3 §1.2 — an exact $1/R$ Coulomb potential with no correction from the hidden coordinates. An atom is a 3D structure being orbited in six dimensions, coupled through a shared plane.

**The spectrum is exactly hydrogen's.** The electron's three observable coordinates see the standard Coulomb problem — same Hamiltonian, same Bohr levels, with the Rydberg assembled from the derived couplings of Part 3. Nothing in the hidden coordinates shifts an energy level, and §4 turns that from an observation into a theorem.

**Orbital shapes are projections of one orbit.** The s, p, d, f shapes are the angular-momentum harmonics of the six-dimensional orbit, read in three coordinates. Their familiar properties become statements about the orbit's symmetry group: the $2L+1$ degeneracies and the selection rules fall out of the sector's rotation structure, and the shell capacities $2, 8, 18, 32$ are the level counts with spin. Two states of the same energy differ not in "where the electron probably is" but in which projection of the definite orbit our three coordinates catch.

**How the shapes are drawn: phase-locked precession.** The shadow picture has a concrete drawing mechanism, and it produces the textbook shapes with integers in the right places. As the electron runs its closed orbit, the orientation of its trajectory in the hidden $\mathbb{CP}^3$ directions slowly *precesses* while its phase sweeps at the orbital frequency $\omega_1$. For a shell of angular degree $L$, the precession rate is $L\,\omega_1$ — the degree enters as the orbit's moment-of-inertia gap — and when the precession locks to the orbital frequency ($\kappa = \omega_1$), the projected path closes into a rose: the radius wobbles $L$ times per turn, the modulus doubles it, and the shadow has exactly $2L$ petals. That is the s/p/d/f lobe sequence $0, 2, 4, 6$, with the degree appearing twice — once as the precession rate, once as the petal count — bound together by the lock. At $L=0$ there is no precession and the shadow is a single ring; a second, independent lock between the breathing frequency and the radial quantum number puts $N-1$ concentric rings on the s shells — the "ring with a dot" of the 2s and 3s orbitals. These rings are not decoration: they are the radial overtones of the standing wave itself — the concentric ripple structure every excited mode carries around its center (Part 1 §1.4) — rendered by the orbit's breathing resonance, with node counts arriving as resonance integers. And this is honest dynamics, not a drawing recipe: the whole construction is the flow of a conserved Hamiltonian on the sector geometry (a Bloch-type precession generated by a fixed Hermitian matrix, whose eigenvalue gap $2L\omega$ *is* the lobe count), verified numerically shell by shell. Two candors attach. The nested shell *sizes* in the accompanying visualization are the standard hydrogenic $1:4:9:16$, supplied for display — the lock fixes the lobe and ring counts, not the radial scale. And whether the dynamics *forces* the lock, rather than the lock being the resonance condition that selects clean orbitals, is open; the lock is where sharp shapes live, but its inevitability is not yet derived.

**What looks like chance is geometry.** The cloud picture, and its irreducible randomness, is the marginal of Part 1 §5.2: a definite 6D trajectory, sampled where it crosses a 3D slice, unconfined in the slice because its binding lives in the sector coordinates. Position measurements on identically prepared atoms scatter because the unobserved coordinates differ — information, not indeterminacy.

**One genuinely new item of ontology, honestly flagged.** The sector's state space contains orbit states with no three-dimensional counterpart — "hidden" components at every shell beyond the first, inaccessible to any 3D probe at any order (a theorem, §4, not a hope). They change no measured number; they are the part of the electron's reality that three-dimensional chemistry never touches. A measured coupling to one would falsify the sector identification outright.

## 3. How 6D chemistry works

Chemistry is where the orbit picture earns its keep, because things chemists must postulate become theorems with the 6D orbit in hand. This section derives the centerpiece on the page.

### 3.1 Hybridization is a basis choice, not a process

Textbook chemistry says carbon "promotes and hybridizes": mixes its s and p orbitals into four equivalent $sp^3$ hybrids at an energy cost repaid by bonding. In this framework the s and p states are *projections of one six-dimensional orbit* (§2), and the sector's symmetry group rotates among those projections exactly, at zero cost — the "mixing" is a change of descriptive basis, as free as rotating coordinates. What physics actually selects is *which* orthogonal set of orbit states the bonding environment picks out. That selection is geometry, and it is fully computable.

### 3.2 The bond-angle theorem, derived

Setup. A bonding electron at a center occupies a directed orbit state — an s share plus a p component pointed along its bond axis $\mathbf{n}_i$:

$$|h_i\rangle = a_i\,|s\rangle + b_i\,(\mathbf{n}_i\cdot|\mathbf{p}\rangle), \qquad a_i^2 + b_i^2 = 1.$$

(That the bound state takes this directed form is itself derived: the companion nucleus's potential is axially symmetric about the bond axis, and on the degenerate shell its leading multipole couples $|s\rangle$ only to the p component *along the axis* — the perpendicular p states carry the wrong angular momentum about the axis and decouple. The bound combination points at the partner.)

Three facts pin the geometry:

1. **Orthogonality is free.** The $n$ bonding electrons form an antisymmetric state (spinor anticommutation, Part 1 §3.4), and an antisymmetrized product depends only on the occupied *subspace* — any basis of it may be taken orthonormal. So $\langle h_i|h_j\rangle = 0$ for $i\neq j$ costs nothing.
2. **Completeness fixes the shares.** The occupied subspace contains $|s\rangle$ (the perturbation builds every hybrid partly from it), so the s shares must resolve it: $\sum_i a_i^2 = 1$.
3. **Equivalence splits them equally.** For $n$ identical ligands in a symmetric arrangement, symmetry permutes the hybrids and fixes every $a_i^2 = 1/n$.

Now compute the overlap of two hybrids and set it to zero:

$$\langle h_i|h_j\rangle = a^2 + (1-a^2)\,(\mathbf{n}_i\cdot\mathbf{n}_j) = \frac{1}{n} + \Bigl(1-\frac1n\Bigr)\cos\theta_{ij} = 0$$

$$\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\;\cos\theta = -\frac{1}{n-1}\;}$$

| bonds $n$ | $\cos\theta$ | angle | realized by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | $-1$ | $180°$ | acetylene, CO$_2$ |
| 3 | $-\tfrac12$ | $120°$ | graphene, ethylene |
| 4 | $-\tfrac13$ | $109.47°$ | methane, diamond |

The tetrahedral angle of carbon chemistry — the shape of methane, of diamond, of the backbone of every protein — from orthogonality and counting, with no empirical input anywhere. And the same Gram-matrix algebra that produces it also *caps* it: $n$ equal-angle directions in three observable coordinates require $\cos\theta = -1/(n-1)$ with $n\le4$, so at most four equivalent bonds exist through s and p states. Five-coordinate centers *cannot* be five equivalent bonds (they aren't — PF$_5$ splits axial from equatorial), and six-coordinate centers must recruit d states, where the analogous computation (shares $\tfrac16,\tfrac12,\tfrac13$ across s, p, d; orthogonality through the Legendre expansion) forces every pair of bond angles to $90°$ or $180°$: the exact octahedron of SF$_6$, again with nothing put in by hand.

Water and ammonia bend away from $109.47°$ ($104.5°$, $107.8°$) because their lone pairs are not equivalent to their bonds — give the lone pairs a slightly larger s share $\delta$ and the same orthogonality algebra yields $\cos\theta = -(\tfrac14-\delta)/(\tfrac34+\delta)$; the observed angles correspond to $\delta = 0.050$ and $0.016$, and the ordering $\delta(\mathrm{CH_4}) = 0 < \delta(\mathrm{NH_3}) < \delta(\mathrm{H_2O})$ tracks lone-pair count as it must. The $\delta$ values themselves are fitted, not yet derived — stated openly.

### 3.3 The rest of the chemical skeleton

The same machinery, more briefly:

- **Shell filling and the periodic table.** Capacities from the orbit-state counts (§2); the *order* of filling (why 4s before 3d — the Madelung rule) from screening in the marginal problem: high-$L$ orbits are excluded from the nucleus by their angular-momentum barrier, see a more screened charge, and float; penetrating low-$L$ orbits sink. Computed with standard screening integrals on the framework's own marginal Hamiltonian, the crossover reproduces the d-block boundaries period by period.
- **Multi-electron atoms.** Electron–electron repulsion is the same Coulomb vertex on shared coordinates — no new coupling. A first-principles variational computation of helium (screened product state, screening $5/16$ an *output* of the repulsion integral) lands $1.9\%$ above the measured ground state, exactly the correlation deficit standard theory prices; nothing hidden-dimensional disturbs it.
- **Aromaticity.** A benzene ring's π system is one orbit spanning six centers; closed-shell filling of the ring's angular modes gives stability at $4n+2$ electrons — Hückel's rule as level-counting — and the circulating current it predicts has the observed diamagnetic signature: inner protons shielded, outer deshielded, with the sign geometry coming straight from the loop.

### 3.4 Why nothing measurable changes: Marginal Exactness

Everything above lands on standard chemistry's numbers, and there is a theorem that says it must. Every potential in the chemical problem separates: nuclear Coulomb terms live on the observable coordinates, the sector well on the hidden ones. For any observable $O$ supported on the observable coordinates — and that is every chemical probe: energies, geometries, spectra, response — the expectation over a product state factorizes,

$$\langle\psi\otimes\chi|\,O\,|\psi'\otimes\chi'\rangle = \langle\psi|O|\psi'\rangle\,\langle\chi|\chi'\rangle,$$

and the sector factor drops out (orthonormality), leaving the three-dimensional marginal dynamics *alone* in charge of every measured number. Since that marginal is the standard molecular Hamiltonian with the framework's derived constants, the framework reproduces tested chemistry identically — not approximately. The one possible leak — the kernel's short-range cross-sector contact — is bounded: its range is the sector width, its strength capped by the sector eigenvalue, and the resulting shift is below one part in $10^9$ of a hartree, beneath every chemical measurement. A century of spectroscopy is inherited wholesale; what changes is what the numbers are *about*.

So "6D chemistry" is three things, cleanly separated: a **foundation** (what standard chemistry postulates — exclusion, degeneracies, hybridization, the angles — derived from the orbit geometry); an **ontology** (definite orbits, hidden orbit states, no dice); and a **guarantee** (Marginal Exactness: no tested number moves). It is not a prediction of new lab chemistry, and this edition does not pretend otherwise.

## 4. What the quantum world is not

The interpretation debates dissolve here because their shared premise — that the quantum state is a probability object in need of an interpretation — is gone. Stated plainly:

**No collapse.** A detection is an interaction with an outcome; the wave continues being a wave. Nothing discontinuous happens to reality — only to the detector's discrete record (§1).

**No fundamental randomness.** Probability is relative detection rate over a definite substrate (Part 1 §5.3). What looks random is information in coordinates the apparatus does not resolve.

**No measurement problem.** Measurement is not a special dynamics grafted onto the theory; it is the same kernel coupling as everything else, restricted to the channels a detector can physically realize. The one honest residue is §1's completion question — *the account of a transition finishing is carried by the imported quantum, not derived* — and that is a calculation gap, not a paradox.

**No many worlds, no pilot wave.** One wave, one manifold, no branching and no separate particle riding the wave: the "particle" aspects are the wave's own localized standing structures, and the classical world of instruments and observers is a derived feature (colour-singlet bricks, Part 1 §5.1) rather than a postulated cut.

**No spooky action.** Bell correlations are real and reproduced; the locality they violate is locality *in three coordinates*, which was never a constraint on a wave whose correlations live in six or more (Part 1 §5.4). No signal outruns light, because every local detection rate is governed by local intensity.

What remains genuinely open in the quantum sector is listed in §1 and Part 6: the completion dynamics behind the one imported quantum, and with it absolute rates from first principles. The interpretation is settled by construction; the open item is a mechanism, not a mystery about what exists.
